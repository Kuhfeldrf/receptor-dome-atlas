Position-first · Human · Evidence-graded

Receptor Dome Atlas

Enteroendocrine signalling in one register, sorted by where things are rather than by what family they belong to. 152 receptors are placed on the cell by position: the narrow apical neck that touches the lumen, the lateral and basal faces that meet only interstitial fluid, and the compartments inside that a peptide cannot reach from outside at all. They are cross-linked in both directions to the 151 peptides the cell secretes or meets and the 34 peptides that sit in the lumen. Every peptide shows which of its receptors exist here and which face they are on.

Seven positions

Membrane polarity is graded separately from expression and from secretion coupling, because the three fail independently. These zones describe position only. Click a zone to filter.

    Four mechanism classes

    Conflating these is how this literature became overclaimed. Every entry in the luminal register carries one.

    Class I

    Apical receptor ligand

    A luminal peptide binds a receptor demonstrably on the apical membrane, with a functional readout. STa→GUCY2C is the only airtight case.

    Class II

    Apical transporter substrate

    The peptide is carried, not bound. PepT1 uptake depolarizes the cell electrogenically, or delivers substrate for intracellular hydrolysis and basolateral CaSR detection. Peptones, Gly-Sar.

    Class III

    Luminal enzyme modulator

    Acts on a brush-border or luminal enzyme rather than a receptor. DPP-4 inhibitory peptides; protease inhibitors that spare endogenous CCK-releasing factors.

    Class IV

    Post-absorptive ligand

    Real pharmacology, but at a systemic or neural target reached after absorption or injection. Most exorphins, ovokinin, β-lactotensin.

    How much structural evidence each receptor actually has

    Every receptor card in this atlas was queried against UniProt's PDB cross-references, and every hit's method and resolution recorded. This counts, for each of the 203 receptors whose accession resolves, how many of its structures are solved at or better than a chosen resolution. Move the threshold and the distribution moves with it: the left-hand bar is the set of receptors with no structure that good, and at 2 Å it holds most of the atlas.

    Three axes, graded separately

    • Position is where on the cell. Graded (a) demonstrated protein localization, (b) inferred from luminal-versus-vascular dosing in a polarity-preserving preparation, (c) assumed and untested. A cell-level positive carries no position information, and STC-1, GLUTag and NCI-H716 are non-polarized, so no result from them can. Position untested is the largest zone at 73 of 152, and the honest home of most of what is written about EEC sensing. Claimed apical holds the six the literature calls lumen-facing where the experiment was never run.
    • Where on that face is a second determination, made far less often. Basolateral receptors carry it: Lateral wall where the domain is resolved, Face only where the face is demonstrated but not subdivided, Inferred where the face itself is argued from where the ligand comes from. Only PIEZO2 reaches the first, by super-resolution imaging; thirteen of the twenty sit at the third. No receptor in this atlas has been localized to the neuropod, which is a conspicuous gap given that the neuropod is where the cell's synaptic output happens.
    • Evidence grade A to F is how well the entry's central claim is established. A demonstrated in a preparation that could have falsified it; B solid but indirect, or one good system; C real pharmacology in a non-gut or non-polarized assay; D single lab, weak controls, or inference from structure; F no data despite the claim being widely repeated. This scale is a construction for this atlas, not GRADE and not Oxford CEBM. Two weaknesses worth stating: it collapses method strength and replication count into one letter, and it grades the best available evidence rather than the balance of it.
    • Secretion coupling is whether activation is known to change hormone output. It fails independently of the others, which is the atlas's most useful property. GUCY2C is the cleanest apical receptor here and has no demonstrated hormone output; CaSR faces the wrong way and has the best secretion evidence of any receptor in it.

    Searching and filtering

    • Search covers the whole atlas. The default All tab searches receptors, secreted peptides and luminal peptides together and bands results by register, so a sequence motif like YGGF returns the enkephalins, the receptors that bind them and the ectoenzymes that cleave them in one pass. Each register searches its full text: names, synonyms, genes, accessions, raw sequences, modifications, position notes, functions, flags and cross-reference targets. The register tabs are a scope, not a requirement; a scoped search says how many matches it is hiding.
    • Every filter sits under a named axis, because the axes are easy to confuse. Position on the cell is location, and only that. Source on a peptide is which cell makes it, not a location on the target cell. Where its receptors are is location, but of the peptide's targets rather than the peptide. Evidence and Content are neither. In All, Entry type separates receptors from peptides, and every card carries its type above the name with a colour-coded edge: receptors take their zone's colour, secreted peptides violet, luminal peptides ochre.

    What the 2026 data pass changed

    • Curated affinities replace assertion. Every receptor card now carries its endogenous ligands as curated by the IUPHAR/BPS Guide to PHARMACOLOGY, release 2026.3, filtered to human targets whose interaction is flagged endogenous. That covers 79 of the 130 receptors and 252 ligand rows, 117 of them with a measured affinity on a negative log scale, each linked to its primary citation. Where the atlas previously said a pairing was strong or weak, it can now show pKi or pEC50.
    • Nine receptors were added from GtoPdb plus two human datasets that post-date the original sweep: Beumer et al., Science 2024, which deleted 22 receptors in human gastric and intestinal organoids and read out hormone secretion, and Lei et al., bioRxiv 2024, which sequenced FACS-purified human colonic EECs. They carry the strongest secretion evidence in the atlas, because the readout is hormone release from human cells with the receptor knocked out. Their membrane position is untested without exception, which is the recurring shape of this field: the function experiment gets done, the localization experiment does not.
    • One entry is a correction. CALCR was listed as a hard exclusion under Not on the EEC, resting on a purified mouse gastric ghrelin-cell array with explicit negatives. Human organoid and tissue data place it in D cells and EC cells. A published negative from one purified population in one species is weaker than it looks when the receptor sits on a different subtype.
    • One refutation turned out to be a missing receptor. Spexin was recorded as a refuted GALR1 ligand, on the correct observation that it does not activate GALR1. The pairing is real; the receptor is GALR2, which was simply absent from the atlas. GALR2 is Gq-coupled where GALR1 is Gi-coupled, so it activates secretion rather than suppressing it, and spexin drives 5-HT release through it. Spexin is also made by neighbouring G and K cells, which makes this a candidate EEC-to-EEC circuit.
    • Two entries gained functional validation. GPR173 is selectively expressed in human colonic EC cells and its peptide agonist phoenixin-20 increases the frequency of serotonin release events, measured electrochemically in real time. IL13RA1 is in both peptide-producing EECs and EC cells by sequencing and by immunohistochemistry on human colon biopsies, and IL-13 raised GLP-1 secretion in colonoids from three donors. Both move to grade A.
    • GtoPdb cannot help the polarity axis. Its cellular_location table exists in the schema and is empty, so the database carries no subcellular localization at all. Its tissue_distribution table has 3,820 rows of which only 14 name an enteroendocrine cell type, though those fourteen were worth having: immunohistochemistry placing FFA4 in enteroendocrine cells of the colon, FFA2 and FFA3 in duodenal L cells, FFA3 in PYY-containing colonic EECs, and the TIP39 receptor PTH2R in gastrointestinal endocrine cells, which lifts PTH2R off the watchlist.

    The systematic sweep, and what it changed

    • Every supplied GtoPdb file was used this time. The first pass drew on the master interactions.tsv alone, which turns out to miss 44 target accessions that the class-specific interaction files and the two curated endogenous-ligand lists carry. Unioning all nine sources gives 306 human accessions with an endogenous peptide ligand, of which 234 were absent from the atlas and only about eight had been examined.
    • Family completeness was the clearest defect. Checked against GtoPdb target families, the atlas carried some members of 31 families and silently omitted others, 94 siblings in total. Twelve entries were added for the cases that tie directly to material already here, including receptors the atlas had explicitly named as the target of a peptide it carries while not listing them: NMBR for neuromedin B, NTSR1 and NTSR2 for neurotensin, neuromedin N, xenin and the dietary peptide beta-lactotensin, BDKRB1 for ovokinin, MRGPRX1 for BAM8-22, and GCGR for glucagon and oxyntomodulin. Most carry no enteroendocrine evidence and say so on the card; a recorded negative is worth more than a silent gap.

The transduction table from the full database dump gives primary and secondary coupling for 74 receptors, and with it a predicted direction: Gs or Gq raise hormone output, Gi/Go lowers it. This is a prediction from coupling and is kept separate from measured secretion, but it makes the sign of a receptor legible, which is what made GPR19 and DRD2 interesting in the first place. Two filter chips select on it.
    • All 188 cross-reference links were audited, a check recorded as done in the previous pass without being run. 101 are confirmed by GtoPdb, 27 are microbial or dietary and so correctly absent from an endogenous-human table, 23 have no GtoPdb target, and 37 diverge. No outright contradiction was found. Most divergences are the atlas being more granular than GtoPdb on peptide variants, such as CCK-22, CCK-5, gastrin-6 and the enkephalin-family peptides. Eighty-one match exactly; the rest are legitimately absent from GtoPdb or differ only in notation, since GtoPdb writes X for pyroglutamate where this atlas writes Q and marks it. Three name matches carried a genuinely different sequence, and all three resolve in the atlas's favour. GtoPdb gives human urocortin 2 as 38 residues and amidated, which is the rat and mouse pattern, where UniProt Q96RP3 annotates a 41-residue chain and states it is not amidated. GtoPdb's human apelin-36 reads PRSG where UniProt Q9ULZ1 reads SRNG. And its hemokinin-1 is the mouse peptide. Two errors in a database of this size is unremarkable and no reason to distrust it; it does mean that slicing UniProt at annotated coordinates and length-asserting against the peptide's own name was worth the trouble.

    The exhaustive sweep

    • Every file was opened and every table read. A full-text scan of the database dump covered 200 tables and 1,116,127 rows, searching every free-text field for enteroendocrine terms. Twenty tables carried a mention. The scan also surfaced a trap worth naming: many GtoPdb functional assays are run in mouse fibroblast "L cells", the Ltk− transfection line, which a keyword search happily confuses with enteroendocrine L cells. Assays for M1, M5, LH, V2, Kv1.5, FZD1 and FZD10 all match on that string and none is enteroendocrine evidence.
    • The invariant now works on names as well as symbols. The first version only caught receptors named by HGNC symbol, so a card writing 5-HT₃ or CCK1R or NK₁R passed straight through it. Re-running at name level against GtoPdb's own nomenclature found the largest single omission in the atlas: HTR3A, the 5-HT₃ receptor. The atlas draws a vagal afferent in its own figure and carries serotonin as its one flagged non-peptide entry, while the receptor that afferent actually uses was missing, because the serotonin card never writes HTR3A. That receptor is why cytotoxic chemotherapy causes vomiting and why ondansetron works. CFTR was the last one caught, and it earns its place for a better reason than completeness: it is the terminus of the only grade-A apical peptide system here, the chain running guanylin to GUCY2C to cGMP to PKGII to chloride.
    • The mirror question produced 23 peptides. Having asked whether every peptide's receptor exists, the sweep asked whether every receptor's endogenous ligand exists. Ninety-nine did not, and the in-scope ones are now in the register: the POMC melanocortins alpha-MSH, beta-MSH, gamma-MSH and ACTH, whose receptor MC1R carries human secretion data while its ligand was absent; C3a, against which any dietary claim at C3AR1 has to be weighed; calcitonin and intermedin for the corrected CALCR; PTH, PTHrP and TIP39; the kinins; oxytocin and vasopressin; CNP and ANP for the natriuretic guanylyl cyclases; CCK-39; GHRH; annexin A1 Ac2-26; and 26RFa and 43RFa. Chemokines, interleukins and growth factors were excluded as a different class of molecule.
    • Six files were opened and found unable to bear on inclusion, which is worth recording rather than leaving as silence. ligand_physchem_properties holds molecular descriptors with no target, species or tissue. The two approved_drug files hold drug-target pairs, a weaker criterion than the endogenous-ligand files already swept. ligand_id_mapping and GtP_to_HGNC_mapping are identifier crosswalks. all_ligands.sdf is 53 MB of chemical connection tables, and an atlas keyed on sequence and receptor identity takes nothing from them.

    The completeness invariant

    • A second sweep caught what the family check structurally could not. The family-completeness check can only see receptors in families where this atlas already holds a member, so a receptor whose entire family was absent stayed invisible to it. Asking a different question, whether every receptor NAMED by a peptide card actually exists as an entry, found eleven that did not. The largest was the tachykinin family: eight peptides in the secreted register named TACR1, TACR2 or TACR3 while none of the three was listed. The exclusion was deliberate and documented in prose, but a reader following substance P from its card had nowhere to land. MLNR was a straightforward miss, motilin having named its own receptor since the first version of the peptide atlas.
    • This is now enforced rather than audited. The build check fails if any peptide names a receptor with no entry, so the class of defect cannot silently return. Two other invariants run alongside it: every basolateral receptor must carry a sub-location, and every cross-reference must resolve to a real anchor. Writing the check was worth more than the seven entries it produced, because the earlier passes had no way to know what they were missing.
    • Four of the eleven are not receptors and are recorded as such: JAK2 and STAT3 are kinases downstream of LEPR, DPP3 is a cytosolic peptidase included for contrast with the atlas's four luminal ectoenzymes, and SLC6A4 is the serotonin transporter that terminates the EC cell's signal. SLC40A1, ferroportin, is the most interesting addition: hepcidin binds it and triggers its degradation, so the peptide destroys its target rather than signalling through it, and it is one of the few genuinely demonstrated basolateral proteins in the atlas.

    Cross-references and sequences

    • 267 links across 84 receptors, covering 96 of the 128 secreted peptides and 24 of the 34 luminal ligands. Each peptide card names the receptors that bind it with a dot for the receptor's zone and a verdict line giving the closest face it reaches. Relations are distinguished because they are not interchangeable: agonist binds and activates; weak is low-affinity, secondary or proposed; substrate means cleaved or transported rather than signalled through, which is what most apical links turn out to be; antagonist binds and blocks, as LEAP2 does at GHSR; refuted records a pairing claimed and then disproven, kept deliberately, because obestatin–GPR39, des-acyl ghrelin–GHSR, Gly-extended gastrin–CCK2R, spexin–GALR1 and nocistatin–NOP are the most-repeated errors in this field. Absence of a link is informative: 32 secreted peptides reach no receptor here, usually because their receptor is real but not on an EEC, or because none was ever identified.
    • Five peptides appear in both registers and are marked as such: guanylin, uroguanylin, enterostatin, trefoil factor 3 and β-casomorphin-7. Their two cards carry different fields rather than duplicating, because the questions differ: the secreted card gives source cell, distribution and function, the luminal card gives mechanism class, luminal survival and source organism.
    • Sequences are the mature human peptide in one-letter code, sliced from the UniProt canonical precursor at annotated PEPTIDE or CHAIN coordinates and length-asserted against the number in the peptide's own name. There are 128 in the secreted register, 34 in the luminal register, and 123 ligand sequences on 63 receptor cards. Modifications are written in rather than implied: <pGlu>, -NH₂, (SO₃H), (n-octanoyl), Ac-, f-, highlighted cysteines for disulfide pairs, and | for a protease's scissile bond. Source organism is stated on every dietary and microbial entry, because species substitution is the commonest error here. Three corrections came out of assembling them: Ac2-26 is 25 residues, not 26; prosaptide TX14A is synthetic, occurring nowhere in human or rat prosaposin; and the angiotensinogen precursor renumbered, putting angiotensin II at residues 25–32 of 476 where older literature says 34–41 of 485.

    Scope, method and what is missing

    • The endothelium is a zone because it is what a basolaterally secreted peptide meets first, and roughly two-thirds of secreted GLP-1 is destroyed before it leaves the gut. Gut endothelium carries DPP4 but, as far as the evidence goes, not GLP1R: its relationship to the incretins is degradative, not receptive, which is the same verdict the apical face gets at the opposite end of the cell. PLVAP's fenestral diaphragms are asymmetric with the fenestrated face pointing at the epithelium, which is the physical reason a basolaterally secreted peptide can enter the blood at all.
    • The screen ran against a supplied classification of 1,128 human receptor proteins. Its own is_intestinal_receptor flag was not used as a filter, because it marks CaSR, FPR1/2, OPRK1, OPRD1, TAS1R3, GPR119, FFAR1, GPRC6A and OPRM1 as False and omits PepT1, SGLT1, TAS2R38, TRPA1 and ASBT entirely, so all 1,128 were treated as the candidate pool. Of 95 undetermined candidates, 8 were included, 81 excluded, 8 left on a watchlist. Most exclusions rest on evidence of absence: two FACS-purified-EEC datasets publish full repertoires with explicit negatives, which is what makes GHSR, CALCR and the tachykinin receptors hard exclusions rather than unsearched ones. They are kept in Not on the EEC rather than deleted.
    • The recurring trap is ligand-in-EEC versus receptor-on-EEC, and it caught four candidates. Neuropeptide W is in 89% of human antral G cells while its receptors are on no EEC; 26RFa is in gut epithelium while GPR103 sits on enterocytes and goblet cells; orexin is made by EECs while both orexin receptors are absent from them; cholesin comes from enterocytes while GPR146 acts on hepatocytes. The cross-references make this visible, since those peptides link to receptors whose zone dot is grey.
    • Experiments worth running: rubiscolins on EECs, since they are δ-selective and δ is one of the two opioid receptors EECs express, and nobody has tested them. The GMP/CMP receptor, which releases CCK from the mucosal side at low dose and has never been identified, which is the single largest hole in the dietary-peptide literature. C3AR1 on EECs, where casoxin C and oryzatensin converge. A defined dietary peptide against a defined TAS2R in human colonic EECs. Apical/basolateral imaging for the six Claimed apical entries. Apical PAR2 and hormone release, now that a bacterial activator is identified in Bacteroides fragilis Bfp1. NPR1/NPR2 polarity, since they are receptor guanylyl cyclases homologous to GUCY2C with interstitial ligands, so basolateral is predicted but untested. And sub-domain localization for any basolateral receptor at all beyond PIEZO2.
    • Provenance. This merges two earlier atlases, one secretion-centric (128 peptides in 15 precursor families), one reception-centric (121 receptors by membrane polarity, with 34 luminal ligands). Both are preserved in full, with their original grouping on every card as Listed under. One result that would upgrade several transcript-level entries could not be retrieved: Beumer et al., Science 2024, 10.1126/science.adl1460. One asserted axis is flagged UNVERIFIED on its card rather than quietly dropped: the SCTR→L-cell→GLP-1 route could not be confirmed.

    How to read this atlas, and how not to

    This is a curated synthesis assembled by an AI system working from public databases and a small number of primary papers. It is not peer reviewed, it is not a primary source, and it should not be cited as an authority. Its purpose is to make a scattered literature navigable and to show you where the evidence is thin, so that you can go to the primary sources and judge for yourself. Every substantive claim carries a PubMed identifier or an accession for exactly that reason.

    The grading scale is a construction

    The A to F grade is specific to this atlas. It is not GRADE, not Oxford CEBM, and not comparable to any published framework. It collapses the strength of a method and the number of replications into one letter, and it grades the best available evidence rather than the balance of it. A grade A entry means one preparation that could have falsified the claim did not; it does not mean consensus.

    Position is the weakest axis, by a wide margin

    73 of 152 receptors have never had their membrane face tested. That is not a gap in this atlas, it is the state of the field. No database carries the answer: GtoPdb's subcellular table is empty, the Human Protein Atlas resolves plasma membrane as a single compartment, and the surfaceome resources say surface or not-surface without polarity. Where a card says a face is inferred from where the ligand comes from, that is reasoning, not measurement, and it is labelled as such.

    The cross-references are hand-curated

    The 267 links are built from a hand-made ligand table plus each peptide's stated receptor, with false positives removed by hand. Each is auditable on its own, and 101 have been independently confirmed against IUPHAR/BPS curation, but a wrong call is possible, particularly among the links marked weak, which carry proposed and secondary pairings alongside established ones. Four links are flagged single source because no second curator carries them.

    Absence of evidence is recorded as such

    An entry saying "no EEC evidence found" means this atlas found none in the sources it consulted. It does not mean none exists. Enteroendocrine expression here rests on three bodies of evidence: two human datasets from 2024 and GtoPdb's tissue annotations. A receptor documented only in a paper those three do not cover would still be missing, and nothing in this atlas would reveal that. Closing that gap needs cell-resolved expression atlases, not more pharmacology.

    Species, and the cell lines

    Much of the underlying work is rodent. Where a human result contradicts a mouse one this atlas says so, and those contradictions are real: human colonic EC cells respond to fatty acids where mouse ones do not, and GPR160 and GPR3 appear human-specific. Separately, STC-1, GLUTag and NCI-H716 are not polarized, so no result from them can speak to membrane face, however often they are cited as if they could.

    Even the curated sources contain errors

    Checking 156 sequences against IUPHAR/BPS surfaced two errors in that database: human urocortin 2 given at 38 residues and amidated when UniProt annotates 41 and states it is not, and an apelin-36 sequence differing from UniProt at three positions. Two errors in a database that size is unremarkable. The lesson is not that the source is unreliable, it is that a single source is never enough, including this one.

    The retrieval layer is not trustworthy verbatim, and this atlas assumes it is not

    Most of the data added in the most recent pass could only be reached through a fetch layer that routes each response through a summarizing model before this atlas sees it. That layer is not lossless. During this pass it silently inserted a single extra residue into three of ten UniProt sequence records, which is exactly the kind of error that produces a plausible, wrong peptide: one such corruption shifted every cholecystokinin fragment by one position and turned CCK-8 into a sequence that does not exist.

    Nothing here rests on that layer's word. Every sequence was re-derived locally from the precursor at its annotated coordinates and checked as a literal substring, three records were validated against UniProt's own CRC64 checksum, and the Human Protein Atlas figures were cross-checked in full against the whole-atlas file: all 286 classified genes and all 251 per-gene lookups matched, with zero discrepancies. The verification is the reason to trust the numbers. The hazard is recorded because anyone rebuilding this atlas by the same route will meet it, and because a reader is entitled to know that the raw pipeline had an error rate above zero.

    On the dietary peptide question specifically

    This atlas began as an attempt to establish whether food-derived peptides could act on enteroendocrine cells the way endogenous ones do. The honest answer it arrived at is mostly no, and the reasons are structural rather than a matter of insufficient study. Two problems recur. The dominant food exorphins are μ-opioid-selective while enteroendocrine cells express κ and δ but not μ. And almost every positive in-vitro food-peptide result requires 100 µM to 1.2 mM, where the one proven apical peptide system works at nanomolar to low micromolar. That three-to-five log gap is the strongest single argument that most reported food-peptide effects on these cells are non-receptor phenomena: transport load, osmotic or calcium perturbation, membrane interaction.

    Nothing here supports a dietary, supplement or clinical claim. This is a map of what has and has not been demonstrated in cells and tissue. It is not evidence that any food, peptide or preparation does anything in a person, and it is not medical or nutritional advice. Several entries describe receptors that are drug targets; that is pharmacology, not a recommendation.

    What this is good for

    • Finding the gap you want to work in. The zones and the three graded axes are designed to make missing experiments visible. Filter on apical plus couples-to-hormone-release and you get eight receptors out of 152.
    • Checking whether a pairing is real before you build on it. Refuted pairings are kept deliberately, because they are the most repeated errors in this field: obestatin with GPR39, des-acyl ghrelin with GHSR, glycine-extended gastrin with CCK2R, spexin with GALR1, nocistatin with NOP.
    • Following a peptide to its receptors and seeing which face they are on. That is the one thing neither source atlas could do, and it is the reason the two were merged.

    What it is not good for

    • Citation. Cite the primary sources, which every card gives you.
    • Settling a question. Where the evidence is one study or one lab, the card says so, and that is the answer rather than a starting point for confidence.
    • Anything clinical, dietary or commercial. See the block above.
    • Assuming completeness. The atlas knows what its sources know. It cannot tell you what they omit.

    Two extraction gaps were found by auditing the extraction itself, and both are instructive. GtoPdb records 164 targets that have no UniProt accession of their own, because they are multi-subunit complexes, and 124 endogenous-peptide interactions point at them. Every join made on accession passed straight over these. In scope for this atlas were the six calcitonin-family complexes, where receptor identity is set by which RAMP accessory protein is bound rather than by a gene: the same CALCR presents as a calcitonin receptor or an amylin receptor depending on its partner. Separately, GtoPdb has no protein ligand type, so proteins are typed as peptides, which is why entries like erythropoietin and the interleukins appear in a peptide-filtered set. Both were corrected; both would have been invisible to a check that only counted rows. One further GtoPdb row was excluded by adjudication in this pass: the database lists parathyroid-hormone-related protein as an endogenous full agonist at the prolactin-releasing peptide receptor, and PRLHR is the only target in the whole GPCR interaction file against which PTHrP appears. PTHrP acts at PTH1R and PTH2R, both of which are in this atlas, so the row is judged a curation error rather than a finding and is not carried. Two comparable adjudications were made in an earlier pass, against UniProt, on urocortin 2 and apelin-36.

Every figure in this atlas is checked by a build-time validator that fails on a dangling cross-reference, an unverifiable sequence, a peptide whose declared length disagrees with its sequence, an orphan entry that does not state its own lack of a receptor, a gene with no expression row, or a structure row that disagrees with the pull it came from. The structure layer is re-derived from that pull at build time and compared record by record, so a resolution shown on a card is the one UniProt published.
